# Real Life

A modern-day town where the economy is real. Every person in town is simulated: they're born, go to school (if there's a
school), graduate or drop out, go to college or learn a trade, get jobs, get paid, pay rent or a mortgage, buy groceries and
clothes and cars, go out for dinner, fall ill, marry, have kids, retire and die. Every business is a business: it hires the
people it needs (if anyone in town has the right qualifications), sets its prices and wages, buys stock from makers in town or
imports it, and keeps its own books. If it can't make payroll it borrows; if it can't borrow it closes.

You're the unseen hand. A new town starts as empty land with the state highway running in from the west: you lay out the
streets, zone the land, build the town hall, schools, clinics, police and fire stations, parks and bus depots, and set the laws. Local families and the town's property firms decide what gets built on the land you zone, based
on what they can see: empty homes, customers turned away, sales nobody in town can make, people looking for work. Everything
is owned by someone who lives here.

Built in Unity 6 (6000.6.5f1) with URP. Every model is built in code (the same approach as Mobland): boxes and roofs
wearing procedural PBR textures, with window atlases mapped bay by bay so offices, flats and shops light up at night, plus
SSAO, soft shadows, a day/night sky and an ACES grade. There are no model assets; the textures are generated by a Python
script in `tools/textures`. The economy lives in a pure C# simulation (`Assets/Scripts/Simulation`) with no Unity dependencies,
so it can be run and tested headless.

## Open it

1. Unity Hub → **Add** → **Add project from disk** → pick this folder.
2. Open it with Unity 6000.6.5f1.
3. The first time it opens, the project creates `Assets/Scenes/Main.unity`, sets up the URP pipeline (`Assets/Settings`) and
   builds a material for every texture (`Assets/Resources/RealLife/Mat`). If anything looks pink or flat, run
   **Real Life → Graphics → Set Up URP Pipeline** and **Rebuild Materials**.
4. Press **Play**. The game builds itself from code, so the scene stays empty in the editor.

The town saves every month and when you quit, to `Application.persistentDataPath/reallife-save-v1.json`.
**Start a new town** is at the bottom of the Laws panel; the old town is kept beside it as `reallife-old-<name>.json`.

## Time and money

One day is about a real week; a month is four days and a year is 48. At 1x a day takes six minutes, so you can watch the
morning commute; at 90x a year goes by in about three minutes. Money is in dollars at realistic monthly rates: a cashier
earns about $2,350 a month, a teacher $4,600, a software developer $8,000, a doctor $14,500. A house costs around $230,000.

## Starting a town

You start with $5M, the highway and nothing else. Run streets off the end of the highway and zone land beside them for homes:
families start moving in straight away, driving to jobs in the towns around (up to 40 + a third of your population can
commute). Houses go up in about a month; until a property firm can take them on, the bank builds them. Expect roughly 60-90
people after the first year, 250-300 after three and over a thousand after ten, if you keep zoning. As people arrive, families here open the shops,
a builder, a property firm, offices and plants on the land you've zoned for them, and someone with savings moves in to start
what the town needs if nobody here can. Commuters take a job in town as soon as one pays about as well, so local jobs grow
the town further than commuting alone. Build the town hall where you want the middle of town to be: shops and offices gather
round it. Each lot needs a road alongside, so the middle of a large block stays empty.

## How the economy works

**People.** Kids go to elementary school at 6 and high school at 12 — if there's a school with room and enough teachers.
At 18 they graduate if they got enough schooling, otherwise they have no diploma. Graduates may go to the community college
for a two-year trade certificate or a four-year degree (and medical school after that, if there's a hospital). Unemployed
adults go back to learn a trade. Every job needs a credential: anyone can be a cashier, but an electrician needs a trade
certificate, a teacher a degree and a doctor a medical degree. A town without schools ends up with jobs nobody can fill.

**Households** share one bank account. Each month they're paid (less income tax), pay rent or mortgage, utilities,
insurance, loan payments and tuition, put some aside, and what's left is spending money: dinners out, coffee, drinks, the gym,
haircuts, clothes as they wear out, a new TV or sofa now and then, some online shopping. Groceries every week; fuel and
car upkeep (below) if they have one. Families with savings and a steady job buy houses on a mortgage; young adults with a job move out
of their parents' place; singles pair up and move in together; couples have children.

**Businesses.** Shops buy stock from makers in town (a food plant for groceries, factories for clothes, electronics,
furniture) when that's cheaper than importing it, and sell to households with sales tax on top. Restaurants, cafés, bars,
gyms, salons and garages sell services. Farms grow crops for the food plant on Farmland you zone (well away from the
factories, if you like); factories export what the town can't buy at world prices. Offices bill clients outside town and are the best-paid jobs, if the town has the graduates to staff them.
Construction companies build everything and do repair work (when the ones here are booked up for months, contractors from
out of town take the overflow at a higher price, which is what brings another builder to town); trucking depots haul goods (without one, outside hauliers charge
more). The bank lends for homes, cars, college and businesses. Every month each business pays wages, utilities, upkeep and
taxes, its owner takes what's above a cushion, and it decides how many staff it needs and what to charge: prices rise when it
turns customers away and fall when stock piles up; wages rise when it can't hire. Every business belongs to a family in town:
the founders put in some savings and the bank lends the rest. When the owners leave or die without an heir, the staff or
another family buys it, or it shuts and the bank sells the building on.

**Cars.** Every car is its own: a small car, a family car or a premium SUV, with its year, mileage, condition and value.
Cars wear with age and the distance they're driven (commuters' most), need a service now and then, and the more worn, the
more often they break down. A broken car goes to a garage in town and waits its turn: garages have so many mechanic-hours a
day, and the parts (always imported — no building here makes them) have to be in stock. A long queue, or no garage, sends
the work out of town at a higher price, and a family without a working car can't drive to a job out of town. When the repair
would cost more than the car is worth, the family replaces it: trade-in, savings, then a car loan. Dealers sell new cars
they order in and used ones they took in trade (their mechanic gets them ready first); poorer families buy used, richer ones
new, and without a dealer here people buy in the next town over. Worn-out cars are scrapped. You see each family's own car
on the road and on the driveway, the stock on a dealer's lot and the cars waiting outside a garage; the inspector shows a
family's cars, a dealer's lot and a garage's queue, and the Economy panel counts cars bought, scrapped and repaired out of town.

**Property firms.** Rented homes belong to the town's property companies, each run from an office downtown by a local family
with property managers and handymen on staff. They build houses to sell and flats to let, buy repossessed homes from the
bank, collect the rent, pay the property tax and keep the places up. When there are none and people need homes, a family
starts one.

**Who opens what.** A new business only opens when the numbers add up for a few months running: enough custom going out of
town to pay a crew and the loan, or shops here that are full, making money and still turning people away. A second builder
opens only when the first has had more steady work than it can handle for months; a second plant of a kind only when the
ones here are well staffed and comfortably in profit. After one fails nobody tries the same thing for a year (two,
after two failures), so you won't see the same shop open and close over and over.

**Growth.** Zone land and the market decides. Families move in while the region has jobs for commuters and there are homes
they can afford; open jobs bring workers from outside if there are homes for them; dear housing makes developers build more;
turned-away shoppers draw shops; people looking for work, or commuting for low pay, draw factories and offices; crops
imported for the food plant draw farms. Your zone demand meters (top left: homes, shops, industry, farms) show what's wanted.

**The town's books.** Income tax, sales tax, property tax, business tax and college tuition come in; public employees'
pay, upkeep of buildings and roads, unemployment benefits and construction go out. You can run a deficit and borrow, at 5.5%
a year.

**The town line.** Money comes into town from exports, office work billed outside, commuters' pay, pensions and outside
investment, and
leaves through imports, utilities and insurance, outside hauliers and retirement savings. The
highway terminal's inspector shows last month's balance.

## Laws (the Laws panel)

Income, sales, property and business taxes; the minimum wage; unemployment benefit; how much of college tuition the town pays;
public-sector pay; and how many teachers, doctors and nurses, police and firefighters the town funds. You can also close the
town to newcomers.

## The code

| | |
|---|---|
| `Assets/Scripts/Simulation` | The whole economy, engine-free. `Town.cs` (state, map, clock), `Town.Day.cs` (the order of a day and a month), `Town.People.cs` (lives, school, migration), `Town.Jobs.cs` (labour market), `Town.Business.cs` (production, trade, books), `Town.Shopping.cs` (household spending), `Town.Housing.cs` (rent, mortgages, the bank), `Town.Development.cs` (developers, construction, the player's tools), `Town.Start.cs` (the starting town), `Defs.cs` (every number) |
| `Assets/Scripts/Game` | The Unity side: `GameController` (clock, tools, save/load), `CameraRig`, `WorldView` (ground, roads, buildings, the countryside), `Atmosphere` (sun, sky, haze, post-processing, night), `Agents` (people and vehicles moving), `Nav` (road routes), `Hud*` (UI) |
| `Assets/Scripts/Game/Art` | Models built in code: `MeshKit` (mesh builder, one sub-mesh per material), `MaterialLibrary` (textured, tinted, coloured and night-glow materials), `Facades` (bay-mapped facade bands, ledges, signs, flat roofs, rooftop plant), `BuildingArt` (every building), `PropArt` (trees, people, vehicles) |
| `Assets/Resources/RealLife/Tex` | Generated textures: albedo, `_n` normal, `_m` metal/smoothness, `_e` night emission. Facade atlases are 4 bays by 4 floors (ground floors 4 by 1); `signs` has 32 rows of shop signs |
| `Assets/Editor/GraphicsSetup.cs` | Creates the URP asset (Forward+, HDR, soft shadows, SSAO), sets linear colour, builds the materials and the texture import settings |
| `tools/textures` | `python generate.py` regenerates every texture (numpy + Pillow). `facades.py`, `surfaces.py` and `signs.py` hold the designs; sign rows must match `SignRow` in `BuildingArt.cs` |
| `tools/SimTest` | Runs the simulation headless for years and prints the economy month by month: `dotnet run -- 10 42` (years, seed). Add `x x schools` to have it build a high school and college like a player would |
| `tools/UnityCheck` | Compiles the game against a stand-in UnityEngine and checks every model: faces point outwards, a material per sub-mesh, nothing flung off the lot (`dotnet run`) |
